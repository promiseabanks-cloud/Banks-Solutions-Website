# How to Price Your Products When Inflation Won't Sit Still

> You raised your prices three months ago. Now your supplier's costs have gone up again, and you're wondering if it's too soon to raise them a second time. Here's the part nobody explains clearly: easing inflation doesn't mean you can relax on pricing. It means something different.

Nigeria's inflation eased to 15.43% in July 2026, the lowest reading since March. That sounds like good news, and it partly is — but "easing" doesn't mean prices are falling. It means they're still rising, just more slowly than before. If you've been waiting for a break where you can quietly stop reviewing your prices, that break isn't coming, at least not this year.

![Street vendor resting beside baskets of produce at a Nigerian market](https://bankssolution.ng/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/img/nigerian-street-vendor-market-pricing.jpg)

## The part that trips people up

Food inflation actually went the other direction — it accelerated to 20.31% in July, up from 17.52% in June. So while the headline number improved, the category that probably matters most to your actual cost of goods got worse in the same month. This is exactly why watching one national number and assuming it applies evenly to your business is a mistake.

## Why this changes how often, not whether

The CBN is targeting 16.5% for 2026, with a 2-point band either side. The naira has actually been fairly stable through the first half of the year, with the CBN forecasting around ₦1,400 to the dollar. Put together, the picture is: inflation is elevated but no longer accelerating out of control, which means your pricing reviews can move from "constant emergency" to "regular discipline" — but regular still means often, not annually.

## The borrowing angle most people miss

Nigeria's maximum lending rate sitting at 35.17% matters here too. If your instinct when costs rise is to borrow to hold more inventory rather than adjust prices, that borrowing is now genuinely expensive — expensive enough that it can eat the margin you were trying to protect. Price adjustment is often the cheaper move than financing your way around it.

## A simple way to actually do this

Review your actual input costs — not the national inflation number — every four to six weeks. Track the specific things you buy, not a headline figure that may be moving in the opposite direction from your own costs, the way food inflation did in July. Adjust in small, regular steps rather than rare, painful jumps; customers tolerate a predictable pattern of small increases far better than they tolerate a sudden 20% jump nobody saw coming.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does Nigeria's easing inflation mean I can stop raising prices?

No. Easing means prices are still rising, just more slowly — you still need to review your pricing regularly, just not in constant emergency mode.

### Why did food inflation go up while overall inflation eased?

Different categories move independently. Food inflation accelerated to 20.31% in July even as the national rate eased to 15.43% — a reminder to track your own input costs, not just the headline number.

### How often should I actually review my prices?

Every four to six weeks is a reasonable rhythm in the current environment — frequent enough to stay accurate, infrequent enough not to confuse customers.

### Is it better to borrow to hold inventory or just raise prices?

With lending rates around 35%, raising prices in small, regular steps is usually the cheaper option compared to financing your way through rising costs.
