# 80% of Nigerian Small Businesses Don't Make It Past 5 Years — Here's What the Other 20% Do Differently

> Eight out of ten small businesses in Nigeria don't survive their first five years. That's not a scare number to get your attention — it's SMEDAN's own data. The interesting question isn't why most fail. It's what the other 20% are actually doing differently.

SMEDAN's 2023 report puts the number plainly: 80% of small businesses in Nigeria fail within five years. It's still the figure most cited today, and the breakdown behind it is more useful than the headline number itself, because it tells you exactly where to look at your own business.

![Smiling shop owner holding up a Sorry We're Closed sign in her clothing store](https://bankssolution.ng/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/img/nigerian-business-closed-shop-owner.jpg)

## What actually kills most of them

Over 40% of failures trace back to something uncomfortably simple: there was never real market demand for the product or service in the first place. Not bad luck, not a tough economy — nobody actually wanted to buy it in the volume needed to sustain a business. 19% report being outcompeted. 17% cite a poor product offering. Layer onto that inadequate funding — too much reliance on personal savings or friends-and-family money that eventually runs dry — high interest rates on the loans that are available, inexperienced management, weak employment practices, no real accounting discipline, and poor marketing.

## The pattern underneath the list

Almost everything on that list traces back to one habit: building first, and checking whether anyone actually wants it second. That's the order most failed businesses run in. The 20% that survive tend to run it the other way around.

## What the other 20% actually do

They validate demand before they scale — testing whether real people will actually pay, not just say something sounds like a good idea. They keep real financial records from early on, even when the business is small enough that it feels unnecessary. They resist over-hiring before the revenue justifies it, which is a harder discipline than it sounds when the business feels like it's growing. And they treat cash flow as close to sacred — not the same thing as profit, and arguably more dangerous to ignore, because a profitable business can still die from a cash flow gap it didn't see coming.

## Where this actually starts

None of this is really about talent or working harder than the businesses that failed. It's about a small number of specific, boring disciplines, applied consistently, starting with the most uncomfortable one: confirming real demand exists before you commit real money to meeting it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the 80% failure rate specific to Nigeria, or is it global?

The figure comes from SMEDAN's own 2023 report on Nigerian small businesses specifically — it's the most widely cited number for the Nigerian market.

### What's the single biggest cause of business failure in Nigeria?

Lack of real market demand — over 40% of failures trace back to this, more than any other single factor.

### Does having more funding solve the problem?

Not on its own — funding without validated demand just means a business fails with more money spent, not fewer of the underlying problems.

### What's the first thing I should check in my own business?

Whether you've actually confirmed real demand, with real customers, before committing further money — it's the root cause behind the largest share of failures.
