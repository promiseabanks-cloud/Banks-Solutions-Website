# The Verbal Agreement Problem: Why Nigerian Businesses Keep Losing Money on Deals They Never Wrote Down

> The deal was solid. Everyone shook hands. Then the delivery was late, the client refused to pay the balance, and you realized nobody had actually written down what 'late' meant, or what happens when it happens.

A lot of Nigerian SMEs run entirely on verbal agreements, WhatsApp voice notes, a template someone forwarded three years ago, and a draft contract that never actually got signed. It works fine, mostly — until it doesn't, and by then the disagreement is already happening, which is the worst possible time to figure out what you actually agreed to.

![Two businessmen shaking hands and smiling in an office after reaching an agreement](https://bankssolution.ng/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/img/nigerian-business-handshake-agreement.jpg)

## Where these agreements actually fall apart

It's rarely the big, dramatic stuff. It's the delivery clause that never precisely defined the deliverable, the timeline, the performance standard, or the payment milestones — so when something goes wrong, there's nothing specific to point back to. It's the missing late-payment penalty. The absence of a staged payment structure, so you've delivered everything before you've been paid anything. No escrow protection. No clear right to suspend work if a client stops paying partway through.

## One specific mistake that causes real disputes

Here's a drafting error that comes up constantly: people write "Lagos courts" into an agreement thinking they've covered themselves, when what actually matters is specifying Nigerian law as the governing law of the contract. Naming a location and naming a governing legal system are two different things, and confusing them is exactly the kind of gap that turns a disagreement into a genuinely expensive dispute.

## What a basic written agreement should actually cover

You don't need a fifty-page contract for most small business deals. You need something that names, in plain terms: what's being delivered and by when, exactly what "done" looks like, how and when payment happens (and what happens if it's late), what happens if either side wants to walk away partway through, and which country's law governs the agreement if there's ever a dispute.

Write it down. Both sides sign it, even if it's two pages. A WhatsApp exchange saying "ok we agree" is better than nothing, but it's not the same thing as a document either side can actually stand on later.

## What this doesn't replace

None of this is a substitute for a lawyer on anything genuinely high-value — a major supply contract, a partnership agreement, anything where real money or real risk is on the line. For those, get one. This is about closing the gap on the everyday deals that currently run on nothing more than trust and a handshake.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a WhatsApp agreement legally binding?

It can carry some weight, but it's rarely specific enough to protect either side in a real dispute — a proper written agreement is a different level of protection.

### Do I need a lawyer for every business deal?

No — most everyday deals just need a clear, specific written agreement. Save the lawyer for genuinely high-value or high-risk contracts.

### What's wrong with just naming "Lagos courts" in a contract?

It names where a dispute might be heard, not which country's law applies to the agreement itself — those are two separate things, and mixing them up is a common, costly mistake.

### What's the single most important clause to get right?

Payment terms — specifically, staged payments and what happens if a payment is late. It's where most disputes actually originate.
