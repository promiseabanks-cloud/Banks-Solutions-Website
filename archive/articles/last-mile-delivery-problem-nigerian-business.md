# Why Your Delivery Riders Are Costing You Customers (Not Just Money)

> The product was right. The price was fair. And the customer still left a bad review — because the rider showed up two hours late, or not at all, or the package arrived looking like it survived a fight.

Thousands of small Nigerian businesses sell almost entirely through Instagram, WhatsApp, and Facebook, and every single one of those sales depends on somebody else actually getting the product to the customer in one piece. That last leg is where a lot of otherwise good businesses quietly lose customers, and it usually isn't about the product at all.

![Delivery rider on a motorcycle with a branded delivery box, parked on a cracked street in Abuja](https://bankssolution.ng/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/img/delivery-rider-abuja-nigeria-motorcycle.jpg)

## The infrastructure nobody controls

Only about 20% of urban roads in Nigeria are paved. A huge number of areas don't have standardized addressing — no consistent street numbers, informal settlements that don't show up cleanly on any map. None of that is your delivery partner's fault, and none of it is yours either, but it's the terrain every delivery happens on, and it explains a lot of the delays that customers blame on "bad service."

Fuel price swings make it worse. When fuel goes up, delivery costs go up, and that gets passed to the customer one way or another — either as a higher delivery fee that makes them hesitate, or a business quietly absorbing the cost and squeezing its own margin instead.

## The cost that isn't financial

A late delivery costs you a delivery fee, maybe. A rider who's careless with a package, or who goes quiet mid-delivery, costs you the customer's trust — and that customer probably tells two or three other people about it before they tell you. That's the real cost of a bad delivery partner, and it doesn't show up neatly on an invoice.

## Picking a delivery partner properly

Uber exited Nigeria after 12 years, which pushed a lot of small businesses toward alternatives — GIG Logistics, Kwik, Gokada, Sendbox, among others. Don't pick the cheapest one by default. Ask how they handle a delayed delivery, whether they insure against damage or loss, and how easy it actually is to reach a real person when something goes wrong mid-delivery. Test a partner with a handful of real orders before you commit your whole catalog to them.

## Setting expectations with customers

Tell customers a realistic delivery window upfront, not an optimistic one you're hoping to hit. A customer told "2 to 4 hours" who gets it in 3 feels fine. A customer told "within the hour" who gets it in 3 feels lied to — even if the actual delivery time was identical in both cases. Manage the expectation, and you manage a big chunk of the frustration before it ever becomes a bad review.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I always choose the cheapest delivery service?

No — reliability, communication, and how they handle problems matter more than the small difference in per-delivery cost, especially once you factor in lost customers.

### How do I reduce delivery-related complaints without changing providers?

Set realistic delivery windows upfront and communicate proactively if something's running late — most complaints come from mismatched expectations, not the delay itself.

### Is it worth using more than one delivery partner?

For many businesses, yes — having a backup option matters when one partner is overloaded, unavailable in a certain area, or having a bad day.

### What replaced Uber for Nigerian businesses after it exited?

Several local logistics options stepped in, including GIG Logistics, Kwik, Gokada, and Sendbox — each with different strengths depending on your delivery volume and area.
